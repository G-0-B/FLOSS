# *Finite and Infinite Games* — James P. Carse: A Deep Reading

***

## Overview

Published in 1986 by James P. Carse — then professor of the history and literature of religion at New York University — *Finite and Infinite Games* is a work of compressed philosophical vision, less an argument than an extended meditation. Written in 101 numbered aphorisms spread across seven chapters, it unfolds a single governing distinction and then follows its consequences into society, culture, nature, language, sexuality, death, myth, and the divine. The result is one of the most quietly radical works of 20th-century philosophy in English — cited equally by game theorists, mystics, educators, technologists, and activists.

The book's opening sentence is its entire thesis: **"There are at least two kinds of games. One could be called finite, the other infinite. A finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play."**[^1]

Everything else follows from this.

***

## Part I — The Core Distinction

### Finite Games: The Logic of Boundaries

Finite games are defined by their limitations. They require:[^1]

- **Temporal boundaries** — a definitive beginning and end
- **Spatial boundaries** — a marked field of play
- **Numerical boundaries** — selected, eligible participants
- **Internal rules** — fixed for the duration of play, agreed upon before play begins

A finite game must be won, and winning is established by agreement among the players. As Carse writes, *"No other condition than the agreement of the players is absolutely required in determining who has won the game."* Even referees and spectators cannot override the players' own mutual recognition.[^1]

The rules of a finite game are contractual and must be published in advance. Crucially, they cannot change mid-game — if they do, a different game is being played. The rules establish who has won. Violating them doesn't just break a rule; it invalidates the contest entirely.[^1]

Finite games are **theatrical** — scripted toward an already-known form of ending, performed before an audience. The winner of a finite game wins a **title**: a public recognition, timeless in character, that one has prevailed. Titles are bestowed by audiences; they cannot be self-claimed. And their power depends entirely on continued recognition.[^1]

### Infinite Games: The Logic of Horizons

Infinite games share exactly one property with finite games: **players must choose freely to play**. *"Whoever must play, cannot play."* Beyond this, the contrast is total.[^1]

Infinite games have no spatial or numerical limits. They are open to anyone who wishes to play. Their time is not *world time* but *time created within the play itself* — so it is impossible to say when an infinite game began or how long it has lasted. Most critically: **the rules of an infinite game must change in the course of play.** Not arbitrarily — but whenever the game is threatened with a finite outcome, players alter the rules to prevent any single player from winning, and to keep everyone in play.[^1]

*"If the rules of a finite game are the contractual terms by which the players can agree who has won, the rules of an infinite game are the contractual terms by which the players agree to continue playing. For this reason the rules of an infinite game have a different status... They are like the grammar of a living language, where those of a finite game are like the rules of debate."*[^1]

Infinite games are **dramatic** — genuinely open, capable of surprise, oriented toward a future that has not yet been scripted. The infinite player moves toward the **horizon**: that limit of vision which recedes with every step, never enclosable within a boundary.[^1]

## Part II — Self-Veiling, Seriousness, and Playfulness

### The Paradox of Finite Freedom

Carse notes a deep contradiction at the heart of finite play. Players must be free to choose their roles — yet they must *forget* this freedom once they begin, or the competitive effort collapses. He calls this **self-veiling**: the intentional forgetting of the voluntary nature of one's participation.[^1]

Using the example of an actress playing Ophelia: she must feel the role as real — yet she can never forget she is acting. Both she and the audience are in *complicity with the veil*. Carse extends this to all social roles. "Persons who assume this role [of mother] must suspend their freedom with a proper seriousness in order to act as the role requires."[^1]

This is not condemned — finite self-veiling makes culture possible. The issue is whether anyone is ever willing to *acknowledge* the veil: *"to openly acknowledge, if only to ourselves, that we have freely chosen to face the world through a mask."* As Sartre is quoted: *"To believe is to know you believe, and to know you believe is not to believe."*[^1]

### Seriousness vs. Playfulness

Carse makes a subtle and crucial distinction. **Seriousness** is not the same as importance. To be serious is *to press for a specified conclusion*, to dread unpredictable outcome. Seriousness is always related to roles and scripts. **Playfulness**, by contrast, is *to allow for possibility whatever the cost to oneself* — not frivolity, but radical openness to consequence.[^1]

*"To be serious is to press for a specified conclusion. To be playful is to allow for possibility whatever the cost to oneself."*[^1]

Infinite players can and do enter finite games — with full energy and appropriate self-veiling — but **they do so playfully, not seriously.** They embrace the abstractness of finite games *as abstractness*, treating roles as roles, never forgetting that what they wear is a mask.[^1]

## Part III — Titles, Names, Power, and Strength

### Titles and Names

What a finite game yields is a **title** — public, timeless, pointing backward at a completed past. Titles require audiences to be effective; they draw attention to what has been finished and remove the titled person from further competitive challenge in that domain. "A title effectively takes a person out of play."[^1]

Names, by contrast, point forward. A name is given at birth — before anything has been accomplished — and opens an indeterminate future. *"When a person is known only by name, the attention of others is on an open future... Our futures enter into each other."* Titles are theatrical; names are dramatic. When public figures become known only by title, their humanity collapses into role: *"When Jesus is regarded by way of a title instead of a name, he becomes an abstracted, theatrical role, a person with whom we can share no future."*[^1]

### Power vs. Strength

Power, for Carse, is a strictly finite concept. It arises only within boundaries, is measurable only comparatively, and is visible only retrospectively — one cannot be sure who is powerful until the game has ended. *"One does not win by being powerful; one wins to be powerful."* Power is theatrical: it requires deference, an audience, a recognition that the struggle in which it was won cannot be re-opened.[^1]

Infinite players do not seek power. They play with **strength**: the capacity to carry the past into the future, to open rather than close. *"A powerful person is one who brings the past to an outcome, settling all its unresolved issues. A strong person is one who carries the past into the future, showing that none of its issues is capable of resolution."*[^1]

*"Strength is paradoxical. I am not strong because I can force others to do what I wish as a result of my play with them, but because I can allow them to do what they wish in the course of my play with them."*[^1]

***

## Part IV — Death, Immortality, and Infinite Mortality

### Death in Finite Play

In finite games, death functions as the **terminal move** — the act that ends play for the loser absolutely. The loser is declared without title, without audience, without voice. Significantly, this has nothing necessarily to do with bodily death. One can be *dead in life* (resigned acceptance of loser-status) or *alive in death* (the "life in death" of those whose titles are so great they must be memorialized forever — Egyptian pharaohs, Christian saints, fallen soldiers).[^1]

The finite orientation toward death produces a contradiction: if life itself is the prize one competes for, then *one is not yet living during the game* — one is competing for life, not living it. *"Life itself appears only as a means to life"* (Marx, as cited by Carse).[^1]

### Immortality as Contradiction

Carse argues that the desire for immortality — preservation of the titled self — is the supreme contradiction of finite play. An immortal soul, stripped of genuine choice, cannot surprise or be surprised. It is a state of *unrelieved theatricality*. The self one seeks to preserve is not a living self but a performed role — *"a permanently veiled selfhood."*[^1]

*"Immortality is the state of forgetting that we have forgotten — that is, overlooking the fact that we freely decided to enter into finite play, a decision in itself playful and not serious."*[^1]

### Death in Infinite Play

Infinite players die — but their dying is offered as a **continuation of play**. *"The death of an infinite player is dramatic... infinite players offer their death as a way of continuing the play."*[^1]

*"The finite play for life is serious; the infinite play of life is joyous. Infinite play resounds throughout with a kind of laughter... laughter with others with whom we have discovered that the end we thought we were coming to has unexpectedly opened."*[^1]

Infinite players choose to be mortal, precisely because mortality keeps the game genuinely open. *"It is a kind of play that requires complete vulnerability."* As Nietzsche is invoked: of the infinite player we can always say *"they die at the right time."*[^1]

## Part V — Society, Culture, and the Distinction That Matters

### Society as Finite Game

Carse defines **society** as the sum of relations under some form of public constraint — all that a people *feels it must do*. A society is a single large finite game containing many smaller games. It has clear boundaries, an enshrined past, a ranked citizenry, and above all a need to maintain its rules. Deviation from societal rules threatens to de-legitimate past winners.[^1]

Property, for Carse, is the instrument by which titles are made visible. *"The purpose of property is to make our titles visible. Property is emblematic."* Property must be both **compensatory** (corresponding to the effort expended) and **consumed** (displayed as leisure, absence of labor) — the famous Veblen insight about conspicuous consumption is deployed here: *"It is apparent to infinite players that wealth is not so much possessed as it is performed."*[^1]

Society depends critically on its artists (*poietai* — Plato's word) to theatricalize property relations and maintain the legitimacy of its titles. But this creates the society's deepest contradiction: those same artists are most likely to remember what the society must forget — *that society is a species of culture.* Powerful societies do not merely silence artists; *"they go to war as a way of silencing their poietai."* Museums, notably, are described as designed *"not to protect the art from people, but to protect the people from art."*[^1]

### Culture as Infinite Game

**Culture**, by contrast, is an infinite game. It has no boundaries, no eligibility requirements, no fixed past to preserve. *"Culture has no boundaries. Anyone can be a participant in a culture — anywhere and at any time."*[^1]

While a society is defined by its **boundaries**, culture is defined by its **horizon**. A boundary is a phenomenon of opposition — it requires hostile forces to maintain itself. A horizon is a phenomenon of vision — it recedes as you move toward it, encloses nothing, admits everything.[^1]

*"Every move an infinite player makes is toward the horizon. Every move made by a finite player is within a boundary."*[^1]

Deviancy is the essence of culture: *"Whoever merely follows the script, merely repeating the past, is culturally impoverished."* Cultural deviancy does not cut the past off — it *continues what was begun and not finished in the past.* This is why "a culture does not have a tradition; it is a tradition."[^1]

The Renaissance illustrates the point: it is not a period but a people — without boundary, without enemy. *"Whoever is not of the Renaissance cannot go out to oppose it, for they will find only an invitation to join the people it is."*[^1]

## Part VI — Time, Training, and Education

### Finite Time vs. Infinite Time

For finite players, **freedom is a function of time**: one must *have* time to be free. Time runs out, diminishes, is consumed. Finite history is theatrical time — periodized, bracketed between curtains, viewable from outside. *"The outcome of a finite game is the past waiting to happen. Whoever plays toward a certain outcome desires a particular past."*[^1]

For infinite players, **time is a function of freedom**: one is *free to have* time. *"A finite player puts play into time. An infinite player puts time into play."* There is no age for an infinite player, because one does not live in another's time. *"There is no such thing as an hour of time. There can be an hour of love, or a day of grieving, or a season of learning, or a period of labor."*[^1]

### Training vs. Education

One of Carse's most practically applicable distinctions: **Training** prepares against surprise — it regards the past as finished and projects that finished pattern into the future. **Education** prepares *for* surprise — it sees the past as incomplete and continues it.[^1]

*"To be prepared against surprise is to be trained. To be prepared for surprise is to be educated. Education discovers an increasing richness in the past, because it sees what is unfinished there... Training leads toward a final self-definition. Education leads toward a continuing self-discovery."*[^1]

## Part VII — Nature, Garden, and Machine

### Nature as the Unspeakable

*"Nature is the realm of the unspeakable. It has no voice of its own, and nothing to say."* The finite response to nature's indifference is to turn it into an opponent — to develop the technology (the **machine**) that commands nature by obeying it (Bacon's paradox).[^1]

But Carse identifies the irony: by claiming to speak *for* nature, the scientist becomes supernatural — standing outside nature to comment on it. The gods, silenced by science, have stolen back in the form of the scientist's magisterial authority. *"If we must obey to command, then our commanding is only obeying and not commanding at all."*[^1]

Nature is ultimately **the source of metaphor**: its perfect irreducibility to human language is precisely what makes language possible. *"At its root all language has the character of metaphor, because no matter what it intends to be about it remains language, and remains absolutely unlike whatever it is about."*[^1]

### Machine vs. Garden

The **machine** is driven by force introduced from without; it requires design, fuel, and an operator. It eliminates surprise. It is aimed at ending the drama of nature by making its behavior predictable and controllable.[^1]

The **garden** is grown by an energy originating from within. A garden is not a *place one lives beside but a place one lives within.* Gardening is *horizonal activity* — it maximizes spontaneity, celebrates variety, and transforms its workers. *"One learns how to drive a car, one learns to drive as a car; but one becomes a gardener."*[^1]

*"Gardening is not outcome-oriented. A successful harvest is not the end of a garden's existence, but only a phase of it... Gardens do not die in the winter but quietly prepare for another season."*[^1]

The machine-garden contrast maps directly onto the finite-infinite distinction. Machinery is used *against* others, *against* itself, and ultimately against ourselves — since *"to operate a machine is to operate like a machine."* Weapons are the machine in its purest form: equipment designed not to maximize play but to end it. *"Killers are not victors; they are unopposed competitors, players without a game, living contradictions."*[^1]

***

## Part VIII — Evil, War, and the Silence of the Unheard

### The Definition of Evil

Carse offers one of his most striking definitions: **Evil is the termination of infinite play.** It is specifically *"infinite play coming to an end in unheard silence"* — not the loss of a player's voice, but the loss of *listeners* for that voice.[^1]

*"Evil is not the attempt to eliminate the play of another according to published and accepted rules, but to eliminate the play of another regardless of the rules... Evil is not the acquisition of power, but the expression of power."*[^1]

The Nazis are the cited example: they did not compete with the Jews for a title — they demanded *recognition of a title without competition.* And since recognition cannot be forced, the only way to achieve it was to silence the Jewish voice entirely: *"They were to die in silence, along with their culture, without anyone noticing."*[^1]

Crucially: *"Evil is never intended as evil. Indeed, the contradiction inherent in all evil is that it originates in the desire to eliminate evil."* Every tidying of history — every claim to bring history to a sensible, final conclusion — is an act of evil. *"It is evil to act as though the past is bringing us to a specifiable end."*[^1]

### War as Boundary-Maintenance

War, in Carse's analysis, presents itself as *necessary for self-protection*, but is actually *necessary for self-identification.* A state with no enemies has no boundaries. *"Under the constant danger of war the people of a state are far more attentive and obedient to the finite structures of their society"* (Hegel, cited).[^1]

Finite players wage war to protect boundaries; infinite players **oppose war because it engenders boundaries.** The infinite player's counter-strategy is not violence but *poiesis and vision* — offering those caught in finite conflict a glimpse of horizonal life. *"Infinite players do not rise to meet arms with arms; instead, they make use of laughter, vision, and surprise to engage the state and put its boundaries back into play."*[^1]

## Part IX — Language, Speech, and the Infinite Address

### Finite vs. Infinite Speech

Finite speech is **about** something that already exists; its meaning precedes its utterance. It is magisterial — aimed at establishing winners over losers in contests of knowledge. *"Knowledge is like property. It must be published, declared, or in some other way so displayed that others cannot but take account of it."*[^1]

**Infinite speech** originates from nothing but the genius of the speaker; it is an *address*, not a command. Its meaning is not predetermined but generated through the response it provokes. *"Infinite speakers must wait to see what is done with their language by the listeners before they can know what they have said."*[^1]

The silence of finite speech is *the silence of obedience* — imposed, the death of play. The silence of infinite speech is *the silence of listening* — generative, the birthplace of new speech. *"Finite speech informs another about the world — for the sake of being heard. Infinite speech forms a world about the other — for the sake of listening."*[^1]

### Explanation vs. Narrative

**Explanation** shows why matters *must* be as they are — it is backward-looking, law-governed, aimed at silencing further inquiry. **Narrative** shows how matters *came to be as they did* — it raises possibilities, invites re-reading, and refuses to close.[^1]

*"Explanations settle issues, showing that matters must end as they have. Narratives raise issues, showing that matters do not end as they must but as they do."*[^1]

Storytellers — genuine *poietai* — do not convert listeners to a superior truth. *"Ignoring the issue of truth and falsehood altogether, they offer only vision."* They return listeners *from knowledge to thinking, from a bounded way of looking to a horizonal way of seeing.*[^1]

***

## Part X — Myth and the Inexhaustible Story

Carse ends the book with a meditation on myth. Myths are not proto-explanations, not primitive science. They are *stories that insist on being stories* — and insist on being told. When we strip them for metaphysical meaning, they spring back out of their own vitality. *"Myths, told for their own sake, are not stories that have meanings, but stories that give meanings."*[^1]

Three myths are offered as exemplars of infinite storytelling:

- **Abraham** — promised descendants as numberless as the stars, a myth of a future that always has a future, "a myth of horizon."[^1]
- **The Buddha's enlightenment** — perfect unspeakability that has generated "an immense flow of literature in scores of languages that shows no signs of abating."[^1]
- **The Christian myth** — "that of a god who listens by becoming one of us... a god emptied of divinity, who gave up all privilege of commanding speech."[^1]

What these myths share is their refusal to close — their resonance with *the paradox of silence.* The more they are interrogated, the more they generate. They are *magic trees in the garden of culture* that grow more fecund the more they are pruned.[^1]

| Time | Externally bounded; runs out | Internally generated; begins with each moment |
